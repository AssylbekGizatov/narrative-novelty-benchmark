A thousand differently worded endings need not create a new possibility of play. This study asks how novelty-loss benchmarks for human–AI game narrative authoring can distinguish textual variation, changed action–consequence relations and reduced consequence breadth. We contribute an executable measurement audit grounded in interactive narrative, rather than treating every structural difference as lost originality.

Using the official Ink runtime, we execute 400 seeded playthroughs of two openly licensed games, retain 234 valid branching contexts and independently replay all 679 extracted transitions. Controlled option removal, consequence collapse and consequence reassignment produce 9,341 nontrivial structural cases, supplemented by 936 controls. We compare asset-level word Jaccard, character-trigram and TF-IDF distances with action-paired response comparisons, aligned-transition Jaccard distance and signed consequence-breadth loss. Further tests examine representation ablations, annotation noise, observation horizons and incomplete sampling.

Asset inventories miss all inventory-preserving structural edits. However, action-paired word comparisons detect every tested prose mutation, challenging a blanket claim that textual measures are inadequate. In the emoji-based game, word comparisons miss consequence collapse and reassignment; character comparisons recover 99.87% and 99.67% of cases, respectively, using context-weighted rates. Permutations preserve consequence breadth despite large assignment distances. In a separate two-outcome simulation, independent samples from an unchanged game produce apparent directional loss in 23.5% of comparisons under a specified rare-outcome condition.

The findings establish concrete measurement failure modes and a reproducible protocol for evaluating narrative version changes. They validate neither a complete originality score nor a causal effect of AI assistance on human authors.
